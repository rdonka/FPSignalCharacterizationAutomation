Fiber Photometry Signal Characterization Automation

Author

Rachel M. Donka

Published

August 21, 2026

Overview

Fiber photometry is a commonly used technique to record in vivo neural activity and neurotransmitter dynamics. Fiber photometry requires the expression of a fluorescent sensor and the implantation of a fiber optic cannula through which changes in fluorescence can be measured in real time. Surgical success rates vary and the classification of subjects with real biological signal is critical to maintaining the quality of collected data sets, which is critical for robust, replicable, and interpretable scientific findings. Subjective assessment of signal presence and quality presents a challenge both in the determination of data inclusion criteria and in consistency between researchers.

The goal of this analysis was to enable automatic classification of individual subject fiber photometry recordings and identify subjects with biological signal versus those lacking biological signal. To do so, I applied an elastic net logistic regression model to establish a robust classification threshold for predicting the presence of a true signal based on extracted features.

Introduction

Fiber photometry is a relatively new technique to record in vivo neural activity through the use of biosensors. Biosensors fluoresce in response to ligand binding, providing users a method by which to assay changes in activity via the collection of relative changes in the intensity of collected fluorescence. Numerous sensors exist to assay cell body activity via calcium activity (e.g., GCaMP), or extracellular neurotransmitter release. To record dopamine (DA) activity, common approaches include cell body recording via Cre dependent GCaMP6f in a TH:Cre rat line, or dopamine sensors expressed downstream in regions like the nucleus accumbens (NAc). Two primary families of DA sensors are utilized (GRAB and dLight sensors), which vary with respect to binding affinity, temporal kinetics, and dynamic range. Across all sensors, recordings of cell body or downstream release activity in the NAc can detect both spontaneous and evoked activity reliably.

In vivo fiber photometry requires surgical preparation to express the fluorescent sensor via injection of a viral vector construct (e.g., GRABDA2h or dLight1.3b) and the implantation of a fiber optic cannula. Surgical success rates vary, as numerous factors affect the ultimate quality of the in vivo signal. Surgical technique is critical to ensure injection success (infusion, placement) and correct placement of the fiber optic implant. Failure of the injector, damage to the fiber optic implant, or off-target implantation can result in lack of signal during in vivo fiber photometry recordings. Additional factors including by not limited to post-operative inflammation, blood flow artifact, and sensor expression can also affect the quality of the signal.

Typically, fiber photometry users subjectively decide if a recording has “signal” by visually inspecting the live or recorded sensor-dependent and control streams. The decision is straight forward in cases where signal is clearly present or not present:

In these cases, determination of inclusion can be difficult. The decision of “signal” or “no signal” may vary both within and between experimenters, resulting in inconsistent signal quality across experiments. Prior to experimentation, it is critical to include subjects with high quality signal and exclude those with low-quality or no signal to maintain the quality and validity of experimental data sets. Identifying if individual subjects display sufficient signal for experimental manipulations is critical to maintaining the quality of experimental data sets for many reasons:

  • Noise: Including subjects with poor quality signal can add noise to the ultimate experimental conclusion, obscuring valid scientific conclusions or potentially resulting in incorrect interpretations (opposite effect, null findings, etc)

  • Long term validity: Photobleaching occurs across repeated recordings in all subjects, which decreases the overall strength of the fluorescent signal intensity. In subjects with poor quality signal at the start of the experiment, photobleaching can entirely eliminate the signal by the end of the experimental paradigm

  • A Priori Inclusion: After experimental data has been collected, the removal of subjects with low quality signal is difficult and ethically complicated. Experimenter bias towards inclusion or exclusion based on the individual subjects results is a significant risk. Even with careful decision making, excluding subjects without an objective criteria is questionable and opens up the project to criticism and skepticism regarding the potential for skewed or cherry picked results.

Thus, classifying subjects for inclusion in fiber photometry experiments prior to the initiation of experimental protocols is critical to maintaining both data quality and appropriate data practices.

Over the course of experimental protocols, inevitably cases will occur where signal is lost in individual subjects due to a multitude of reasons including photobleaching, inflammation, issues with headcap viability, and duration of protocol. Additionally, individual sessions may fail to record viable signal due to issues with the patch cord, connection between the patchcord and fiber optic ferrule, or animal behavior resulting in degradation of the connection. In these circumstances, identifying an objective inclusion/exclusion criterion to apply to filter session recordings for inclusion is particularly critical.

The overall goal of this project was to create a model capable of automatically classifying in vivo fiber photometry recordings of dopamine activity as having signal or no signal, and provide a binary inclusion/exclusion decision. To do so, I first hand scored in vivo fiber photometry recordings from subjects with a variety of DA sensors (VTA GCaMP6f, NAc GRABDA2H, NAc dLight1.3b) as having signal or no signal, and scored the quality of the signal from 0 to 3. For each recording, numerous variables were quantified to charachterize the signal including descriptive statistics (mean, range), frequency domain magnitudes, and ratios between sensor-dependent and control streams. The dataset was split into training and testing data sets, and an elastic net model was trained and tested for accuracy compared to hand-scored classifications. Signal quality ratings were used to evaluate model accuracy and elucidate model classification errors.

Elastic net was chosen as the optimal approach for several reasons:

  • Multicollinearity Handling: Quantified parameters may display significant pairwise collinearity by nature (i.e., multiple measures of variance, variance effects on frequency spectrum). Elastic net introduces a grouping effect that allows highligh correlated predictors to enter or leave the model together with proportional coefficient weights which stabilizes feature selection.

  • Preventing Overfitting: The elastic net model penalizes model complexity and contracts coefficients towards zero, which reduces model variance while maintaining low bias.

  • Sparsity: Redundant or uninformative metrics are automatically set to zero, extracting a concise set of features that contribute to accurate classification.

  • Customizable Decision Thresholds: The model outputs predicted probabilities which can be converted to discrete classifications. Classification thresholds can be tuned to prioritize sensitivity (minimizes false negatives) or specificity (minimizes false positives) depending on goals.

Here we present the results of an elastic net model to automatically classify in vivo dopamine fiber photometry recordings as displaying signal or no signal. Overall, the model was highly accurate and produced consistent accurate classification of recordings based on extracted features of the signal-dependent, background, and processed data streams.

Methods

All subjects in this data set were prepared for other experimental protocols. Recordings were conducted prior to the start of experimental protocols.

Surgical Preparation

Subjects were prepared for fiber photometry experiments with injection of viral vectors to express one of three fluorescent constructs: GCaMP6f in ventral tegmental area (VTA) dopamine (DA) cell bodies (TH:CRE rat line), dLight1.3b in the nucleus accumbens (NAc; lateral shell or core), or GRABDA2H in the NAc (lateral shell or core). Subjects were monitored postoperatively for a minimum of 10 days.

Fiber Photometry Recordings

Fiber photometry recordings were conducted a minimum of three weeks post-surgery to allow for stabilization of sensor expression. Recordings were conducted in standard operant conditioning chambers (house light and fan on) with RZ5 or RZ10 processors and the software Synapse (Tucker Davis Technologies). Sensor-dependent signal was measured via a 465 nm LED, while a control stream was recorded via a 405 nm LED. A minimum of 5 minutes of recording was collected for each subject.

Signal Classification

Fiber photometry recordings were hand scored as having ‘Signal’ or ‘No Signal’ based on prior experience and subjective evaluation. Recordings were also assigned a quality score from 0 to 3, with 0 meaning no signal, 1 meaning low quality signal that should be classified as no signal, 2 meaning moderate signal of sufficient quality to be scored as having signal, and 3 meaning high quality signal.

Each recording was scored two times independently, and discrepancies were reconciled based on the signal quality ratings assigned in both scores.

Signal Processing

Fiber photometry data were extracted and processed in MATLAB (2026a) as in Donka et al, 2025. Briefly, raw data streams were extracted. The background stream (405nm) was scaled to the signal stream (465nm) based on a constant factor derived from the ratio of the power within a high frequency band optimized by sensor (e.g., 10 to 100 Hz). The subtracted data stream was produced by subtracting the scaled background from the raw signal to control for photobleaching and motion artifact effects. The final filtered data stream was produced by applying a band pass Butterworth filter (High pass: 0.0051 Hz; Low pass: 5 Hz) to the subtracted data stream.

Feature Quantification

For feature quantification, fiber photometry streams were divided into halves (2.5 minutes each), and variables were quantified for each 30 second bin within the separated recordings. Averages across bins were calculated to provide one estimate for each recording.

Fiber photometry recordings were quantified by a variety of parameters including stream range, root mean squared (RMS), mean, and power spectrum magnitude (low, medium, and high frequency bands). Quantification was performed for raw signal, raw background, scaled background, subtracted signal, and subtracted and filtered streams. Additionally, ratios between values in subtracted and scaled background streams were included across variables.

Quantified variables were exported to a .csv file and all further analysis was conducted in R.

Model Specification: Elastic Net Classification

To classify whether fiber photometry recordings contained a biological signal (Y/N), an Elastic Net logistic regression was trained. Elastic Net applies a combination of Ridge (L2) and Lasso (L1) penalities (\(\alpha = 0.5\)) to handle correlated predictors while inherently performing feature selection.

The model was trained on the following structure:

Predictors (Features): 30 extracted signal metrics, encompassing raw, background-subtracted, and scaled variables across amplitude and frequency domains.

Target (Outcome): The binary `Signal` variable (Yes / No).

Validation: The dataset was partitioned into training and testing sets using a stratified split, ensuring that signal classifications and sensor types (e.g., GCaMP6f, dLight1.3b) were equally balanced across both sets. To identify the optimal regularization parameter (lambda), the model utilized 10-fold cross-validation on the training set. The final model was evaluated on the test set, converting predicted probabilities into binary classifications using a standard 0.5 decision threshold.

Code 
# ELASTIC NET - SIGNAL -----
## Prepare train and test data -----
# Remove extra columns
ENdata <- modeldata

# Create training and testing sets
ENdata_train <- ENdata %>% filter(Set == "Train") %>% select(-Set, -Split)
ENdata_test  <- ENdata %>% filter(Set == "Test")%>% select(-Set, -Split)

# Create design matrix of predictors (excluding SubjectID)
EN_x_train <- model.matrix(~ . -1 -Signal -SignalQuality -SubjectID -Sensor, data = ENdata_train)
EN_y_train <- ENdata_train$Signal

# Prepare test
EN_x_test <- model.matrix(~ . -1 -Signal -SignalQuality -SubjectID-Sensor, data = ENdata_test)
EN_y_test <- ENdata_test$Signal

## Fit elastic net -----
set.seed(123)

EN_cv <- cv.glmnet(
  x = EN_x_train,
  y = EN_y_train,
  family = "binomial",
  alpha = 0.5,             
  type.measure = "class",
  nfolds = 10 # 10-fold cross-validation
)

# Set lambda 
EN_minlambda <- EN_cv$lambda.min

# Refit model at this lambda
EN_finalmodel <- glmnet(EN_x_train, EN_y_train, family = "binomial", alpha = 0.5, lambda = EN_minlambda)

## Predict test set -----
# Predict probabilities
EN_test_probs <- predict(EN_finalmodel, newx = EN_x_test, type = "response")

# Convert to binary predictions using threshold of 0.5
EN_test_preds <- ifelse(EN_test_probs > 0.5, "Y", "N") %>% factor(levels = c("N", "Y"))

# Predict class
EN_pred_class <- predict(EN_cv, newx = EN_x_test, s = "lambda.min", type = "class")

# Predict probability
EN_pred_prob <- predict(EN_cv, newx = EN_x_test, s = "lambda.min", type = "response")

# Confusion matrix
EN_confusion <- table(Predicted = EN_test_preds, Actual = EN_y_test)

accuracy <- mean(EN_test_preds == EN_y_test)

Model Results

Overall, the specified elastic net model successfully characterized fiber photometry recordings into ‘Signal’ and ‘No Signal’ categories based on quantified parameters extracted from the data streams. Model accuracy was 89.91%. Of the 32 variables included in the model, 23 were significant predictors of Signal or No Signal classifications.

Optimal Regularization Parameter

To identify the optimal regularization parameter (lambda) for the model, 10-fold cross-validation was performed on the training data set. Penalties exceeding the the threshold (lambda.min) resulted in an increase in misclassification. The lambda min value was used to finalize model weights.

Effect of Signal Quality on Signal Classification

To determine if classification errors were driven by signal quality, model accuracy was evaluated across hand-scored signal quality scores (0-3; 0-1 = ‘No Signal’, 2-3 = ‘Signal’). Incorrect classifications decreased as signal quality increased, with the greatest number of incorrect classifications occurring with signal quality ratings of 2, which reflect fiber photometry recordings where signal was evaluated to be visible but mid quality (Fig. 4A). Across signal quality ratings, relative accuracy was above 80%, and reacher 99% for signal quality ratings of 3 (Fig. 4B). The variance of predicted probabilities was greatest across recordings with a signal quality score of 2.

To statistically test differences in model performance across signal quality scores, I performed a binomial logistic regression (Correct ~ Signal Quality) and Tukey-adjusted pairwise comparisons, which indicated significant differences in accuracy between recordings with signal quality scores of 0 vs 3 and 2 vs 3. No other differences in accuracy between quality ratings were observed.

Effect of Sensor on Signal Classification

To determine if sensor type (VTA GCaMP6f, NAc dLight1.3b, or NAc GRABDA2H) significantly affected model accuracy, signal classification in the test set was stratified by sensor type. Overall, accuracy was comparable across sensors and well exceeded the 50% threshold of random chance, with the lowest accuracy percent at 87.5% for GRABDA2H (Fig. 5A).

Absolute values of counts of incorrect and correct model predictions were statistically compared. No significant effect of sensor was revealed by Fisher’s Exact Test for Count Data (Fig. 5B). Evaluation of the distributions of predicted probabilities by sensor indicated reliable predictions of both ‘No Signal’ and ‘Signal’ across all three sensors, but the distribution of predictions for GRABDA2H ‘No Signal’ recordings was wider than for GCaMP6f and dLight1.3b (Fig. 5C).

Model performance across signal quality ratings was highly comparable across sensors, with the highest level of accuracy observed for signal quality scores of 3. Accuracy was lower for GRABDA2H recordings with signal quality scores of 0, potentially driven by a greater degree of sensitivity to DA binding or extended temporal kinetics relative to GCaMP6f and dLight1.3b. However, the decreased accuracy with GRABDA2H in recordings of signal quality 0 was still significantly above chance (Fig. 5D). Decision thresholds may be optimally tailored by sensor to mitigate the incorrect classification of data streams without signal via increasing the strictness of the predicted probability threshold for ‘Yes’ signal classification.

Together, these results indicate that the included predictors capture reliable profiles that can be applied to classify fiber photometry recordings of in vivo DA activity independent of individual sensor kinetics.

Discussion

Overall, the optimized elastic net model described here provided a highly robust and reliable classifer to categorize fiber photometry recordings as ‘No Signal’ or ‘Signal’ across DA sensors based on extracted parameters. The model demonstrated that frequency spectrum magnitude ratios between raw signal and scaled background streams were the most critical indicators of recordings with real biological signal, while the raw RMS ratios were the strongest predictors of recordings lacking biological signal. The model achieved high global accuracy of 89.9%, and post hoc analyses revealed the highest degree of performance in the classification of high quality ‘Signal’ recordings. The model displayed similar accurcy across DA sensors, allowing for robust classification irregardless of differences in sensor properties and kinetics.

Discussion Points

Overall, the model provides a consistent method by which to determine inclusion and exclusion of experimental subjects. To effectively apply this in the lab, I reccomend we adopt a lab wide agreement on procedures and standardize across experimenters to ensure consistency.

  • Threshold for Inclusion: While the model was initially tested with a 0.5 threshold (> 0.5 = Signal, < 0.5 = No Signal), it may be optimal to alter the threshold to prioritize the minimization of the false positive rate (rating recordings as ‘Signal’ incorrectly). A higher threshold may result in greater exclusion numbers but could be more conservative and better able to maintain a standard of high quality data.

  • Testing Timepoints: Typically sensor expression stablizes ~4 weeks post surgery. Signal checks may be performed as often as 2 weeks post-surgery, but as expression increases the signal can markedly change. It would be best to adopt a consistent lab-wide policy for the delay from surgery to signal checking / experiment initation to further optimize inclusion criteria.

  • Application to Existing Datasets: For existing data sets, the model could be applied to determine which subjects to entirely exclude, or if a subject loses signal across the course of treatment. However, in this case it is critical for the lab to decide on an a-priori threshold before testing the individual subjects, as excluding data that has already been collected and analyzed is ethically complicated. If we want to test this out, I propose that we would be best off starting with a data set where there are known subjects that appear to have low quality or lost signal, hand scoring all of the recordings, and then testing the model to determine the accuracy.

Conclusion

Ultimately, this pipeline offers a scalable unbiased alternative to manual visual scoring, and may be used to objectively determine inclusion and exclusion criteria of subjects in experimental data sets, offering a method by which to standardize data quality control procedures and mitigate between experimenter variability in subjective assessments of biological signal presence and quality.
